Why this matters · ليه ده مهم

English

Most of what you've learned is invisible outside your team. An article, a talk or a merged PR turns it into something other people can read and check, and producing one tests whether you really understood the thing. A common pattern in interviews is to ask about this too: what you've published or contributed, and how you approach a codebase you don't know.

Treat what follows as advice, and the mid-level versus senior contrasts as teaching patterns, not measured facts. The README says that 6-10 deep articles get examined more than any certificate. That's this curriculum's opinion, not a measured result. Will Larson's guide on being visible, written for Staff-plus engineers, is more careful: there are "many successful Staff-plus engineers with no external presence, but many find external visibility contributes to their career". He also warns that "Visibility is a transient currency", while learning is a permanent one.

The team, names and numbers in the scenarios are invented for teaching. You're on the Orders team at a mid-size online store, with Mona (product manager), Hany (engineering manager), Karim (mid-level engineer), Salma (junior engineer), Nour (infra engineer) and Yasmin (support lead).

بالعامية

معظم اللي اتعلمته مش باين بره فريقك. المقال أو الـ talk أو الـ PR المقبول بيحوّله لحاجة الناس تقدر تقراها وتتأكد منها، وعملها نفسه بيختبر إذا كنت فاهم فعلًا. ومن الأسئلة الشائعة في الـ interviews: نشرت إيه أو ساهمت في إيه، وبتتعامل إزاي مع codebase متعرفوش.

خد اللي جاي على إنه نصايح، والمقارنات بين الـ mid-level والسنيور أنماط للتعليم مش حقايق متقاسة. الـ README بيقول إن 6–10 مقالات عميقة بتتفحص أكتر من أي شهادة. وده رأي المنهج ده، مش نتيجة متقاسة. والـ guide بتاع Will Larson عن الـ visibility، وهو مكتوب للـ Staff-plus، أدق في الكلام: فيه "ناس Staff-plus كتير ناجحين ومالهمش أي وجود بره، بس كتير بيلاقوا إن الظهور بره بيساعد في مسيرتهم". وبيحذر كمان إن الـ visibility "عملة مؤقتة"، أما إنك تتعلم فدي العملة الدايمة.

الفريق والأسماء والأرقام في المواقف دي متألفة عشان الشرح. إنت في فريق Orders في متجر online متوسط الحجم، ومعاك Mona (الـ product manager)، Hany (الـ engineering manager)، Karim (مهندس mid-level)، Salma (مهندسة junior)، Nour (مهندس infra)، وYasmin (مسؤولة الـ support).

Part 1 — An article worth reading 🔴 Advancedالجزء 1 — مقال يستاهل يتقري

English

The situation. You and Karim fixed a real bug: during a rolling deploy, two outbox relays ran at once, read the same rows, and customers got "order confirmed" twice. You want to write about it.

What a mid-level engineer does. Writes "What is the outbox pattern?", which explains a concept that's already explained in many places, including the outbox lesson in this curriculum. Nothing in it could only have come from them.

What a senior does. Writes the thing only they can: the problem, the experiment, the numbers, and what they'd do differently. The reader gets a bug they can check for in their own relay.

Why it works. A tutorial competes with the docs and usually loses. A measured story has no competition, because nobody else ran your experiment. Writing it also tests your understanding: a first explanation that sounded right often fails once you try to reproduce it. In this story, the first fix missed the crash between publish and commit, and the article says so. The outline below is what a real one looks like, and the weak and strong openings are side by side in Common mistakes. How this counts as proof for the job market is covered in section 16 · 3.4.

# Two relays, one row: how a rolling deploy duplicated our order messages

Reader: backend engineers running a Postgres outbox with more than one relay.
Promise: by the end you'll know whether your relay has this bug.
Not in this article: what an outbox is. One line and a link.

## 1. The problem, in numbers
In one week, about 0.6% of order-confirmed messages went out twice.
Every duplicate fell inside a deploy window.
(Production figures stay as ratios. See the publishing checklist.)

## 2. The setup (4 sentences, 1 diagram)
POST /orders writes the order and an outbox row in one transaction.
A relay reads unsent rows, adds jobs to the notifications queue,
marks the rows sent.

## 3. The hypothesis
During a rolling deploy two relays overlap, and both read the same rows.

## 4. The experiment (test database, 10,000 outbox rows, 2 relays)
- No locking: 1,870 duplicate jobs.
- FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED: 0 duplicates.
- Then I killed the relay between publish and commit, 50 times:
  50 duplicates. The fix wasn't finished.
- Added the outbox row id as the BullMQ jobId: 0 duplicates.
  A second add is ignored while the first job still exists in Redis.

## 5. The result
Deployed the locking and the jobId together: 0 duplicates in 14 days.

## 6. What I'd do differently
- Set the jobId from day one. An outbox is at-least-once. Plan for it.
- Put a duplicate-rate metric on the dashboard before the first deploy.

## 7. What I didn't test
Ordering between relays. SKIP LOCKED gives it up. Our consumer doesn't need it.

Target: about 1,200 words, 1 diagram, numbers checked against report.md.

Part 2 — What you're allowed to publish 🔴 Advancedالجزء 2 — إيه اللي ينفع تنشره

English

The situation. Your best material comes from work, and that's the problem: a real EXPLAIN plan can show customer values in its Filter lines, and a screenshot of a dashboard can show order volumes and hostnames.

What a mid-level engineer does. Pastes the real query plan, customer names included, and publishes because it felt harmless.

What a senior does. Asks first, before drafting, and strips details. They tell their manager what the post will and won't include, rebuild the evidence on a test database, and run a checklist before publishing.

Why it works. Asking first means a no costs you an hour, not a week of writing. Showing the boundaries up front makes the yes easier, because your manager can see what's at risk. And rebuilding the example on test data means there's nothing real to leak. This is practical advice, not legal advice. Your employment contract and your company's rules decide, so read the confidentiality and intellectual-property parts before you ask.

Part 3 — Giving a talk 🔴 Advancedالجزء 3 — تقدّم talk

English

The situation. Hany asks whether someone can explain to the team how the payment-webhook handler survives duplicate deliveries. You say yes: 20 minutes, Thursday, internal first.

What a mid-level engineer does. Builds 30 slides full of bullets and reads them out. People stop listening at slide 4, and the one thing they needed gets lost.

What a senior does. Picks one idea, one story, one diagram, and a demo with a recording as backup, then rehearses out loud three times. If the talk lands internally, the same material can go to a meetup after it passes the checklist from Part 2.

Why it works. One claim and one story are easier to follow and repeat than a list, so give them one of each. The rehearsals are where the real editing happens: the first one tells you it runs 31 minutes, and the second tells you which part your colleague didn't follow. The handler's mechanics are in the idempotency lesson, so the talk doesn't need to teach all of it. Treat this as advice from how talks usually go.

# Talk: How our webhooks survive duplicates
Orders and support, 20 minutes + 5 for questions, Thu 16:00.

## One idea
The provider will send the same event twice,
so the second delivery has to do nothing.

## One story (3 minutes)
In March we answered slowly, so the provider retried a batch.
61 orders were processed twice and 2 customers got two invoices.

## One diagram
provider --event e_91--> payment-webhook --> INSERT event_id (UNIQUE)
                                               new?       process it
                                               duplicate? return 200, stop

## Demo (4 minutes, laptop against a local database)
1. Send one event with curl. Row count: 1. Order: paid.
2. Send the same event again. Row count: 1. Response: 200.
3. Stop the worker halfway and retry. Order is still paid once.

Backup: a 90-second recording, already in the deck.

English

The situation. Your invoice-pdf worker uses an open-source PDF table library (call it acme-pdf-table, an invented name). An invoice whose rows exactly fill the page gets an extra page with one row on it.

What a mid-level engineer does. Opens a drive-by "fix" with no issue: 1,200 changed lines across 14 files, because their editor reformatted them, and a title that says "fix bug".

What a senior does. Reproduces it in a few lines outside the app, searches existing issues, reads CONTRIBUTING, keeps the change small and adds a test. They open an issue first when the change is more than trivial, because a maintainer might already have a reason for the current behaviour.

Why it works. Maintainers' time is the scarce thing, and a prepared PR spends very little of it. A minimal reproduction lets them confirm the bug in a minute, and a test proves the fix and keeps it fixed. Open Source Guides says to check the contributing docs before you open anything, and to open an issue first for a substantial change. GitHub's docs cover the fork flow.

When nobody answers. The same guide says that after "over a week" it's fair to politely ask in the same thread, without contacting anyone privately, and that nobody may ever respond. For a small volunteer project, give it a couple of weeks, ping once, then move on and spend your effort elsewhere.

On AI. Don't send a PR that an AI wrote and you can't defend line by line. Treat that as advice about respect for someone else's time. Some projects go further: QEMU's contributor docs say the project will decline contributions believed to include or derive from AI-generated content. Check CONTRIBUTING for any such rule before you start.

Solution · الحل A worked example from the invented team. Karim's outline on the coupon-validation endpoint, built only from a test database: Problem: p95 of 420 ms at 200 requests per second on the test setup. Cheat sheet · الخلاصة

  • Write what only you can: problem, experiment, numbers, what you'd do differently. A tutorial competes with the docs.
  • "6-10 articles beat any certificate" is the curriculum's opinion. Three real ones beat ten thin ones.
  • Ask before you draft. A no should cost an hour, not a week.
  • Publish checklist: no customer data, secrets, hostnames or unreleased plans; numbers changed or ratios only; contract read. Not legal advice.
  • If the answer is no, rebuild it in the side project and keep the experiment.
  • A talk is one idea, one story, one diagram, a demo with a recording, and three rehearsals out loud.
  • First PR: minimal reproduction, search existing issues, read CONTRIBUTING, small diff, a test. Open an issue first if the change is substantial.
  • Silence: one polite ping in the thread after a week or two, then move on.
  • Don't send an AI-written PR you can't defend line by line, and check whether the project has a rule on it.
  • Plan 3 articles, 1 PR and 1 talk over 12 weeks at about 2 hours a week. Done means published.
