Why this matters · ليه ده مهم

English

The news feed is the classic read-heavy, skewed-data problem. It forces the central trade-off of system design into one decision: pay at write time or pay at read time. The same reasoning applies to any "show me the latest things from things I care about" feature: an activity log, a B2B audit stream, a dashboard of recent orders per customer. The interesting part isn't knowing the words push and pull. It is being able to say where the crossover is, with numbers, and being honest about what those numbers leave out.

بالعامية

الـ news feed هو المثال الكلاسيكي لمشكلة read-heavy والـ data فيها skew. بيجبرك تختار القرار الأهم في الـ system design في حتة واحدة: تدفع وقت الكتابة ولا وقت القراية. ونفس التفكير بينطبق على أي feature شكلها "وريني آخر الحاجات من اللي يهمني": activity log، audit stream لـ B2B، dashboard بأحدث الطلبات لكل عميل. المثير مش إنك تعرف كلمتين push و pull، المثير إنك تقدر تقول نقطة التقاطع فين بالأرقام، وتكون صادق في اللي الأرقام دي مش بتغطيه.

English

Requirements. Functional: publish a post, follow and unfollow, and read a home feed that is mostly recent, with ranking, muted accounts honoured, and deleted posts gone. Out of scope: media pipelines, comments, the ranking model itself. Non-functional: feed p99 under 200 ms, read-heavy, a post reaching most followers within seconds, and a celebrity post not delaying anyone else's post. Eventual consistency is fine: seeing a post a few seconds late is acceptable, and that one sentence is what makes push possible.

Numbers. From the calculator below: 34,722 feed reads/s at peak, and 694 new posts/s. A pure push does 139k timeline writes/s at peak from the average alone, and a pure pull does nearly 7 million Redis reads/s. Note the assumption hiding in "posts × 200": the average number of followers equals the average number of follows, because every edge has two ends. The average is only a starting point, though, and Part 3 shows how far skew moves it. Timeline cache: 7.0 TB for 800 entries each, using a measured timeline of about 69.6 KB; the post store is 3 TB a year before replicas and media.

Storage choice. Posts and follows are source of truth in Postgres; timelines are a derived, rebuildable cache in Redis, so losing them hurts latency, not data. follows needs both directions indexed: forward to list who I follow for the pull path, reverse to list my followers for fan-out.

بالعامية

الـ requirements. الـ functional: نشر بوست، follow و unfollow، وقراية home feed أغلبه أحدث حاجة، مع ranking، واحترام الـ mute، والبوستات المتمسوحة تختفي. بره الـ scope: الـ media، والكومنتات، وموديل الـ ranking نفسه. الـ non-functional: الـ feed p99 أقل من 200 ms، read-heavy، البوست يوصل لمعظم المتابعين في ثواني، وبوست مشهور ميأخرش بوست حد تاني. والـ eventual consistency مقبولة: تشوف البوست متأخر كام ثانية مش مشكلة، والجملة دي هي اللي بتخلّي الـ push ممكن.

الأرقام. من الـ calculator: 34,722 قراية في الثانية peak، و694 بوست جديد في الثانية. الـ push الكامل بيعمل 139 ألف كتابة timeline في الثانية peak من المتوسط لوحده، والـ pull الكامل تقريبًا 7 مليون قراية Redis في الثانية. ولاحظ الفرضية المستخبية في "posts × 200": متوسط الـ followers بيساوي متوسط الـ follows لأن كل edge ليها طرفين. بس المتوسط نقطة بداية بس، والجزء 3 بيوريك الـ skew بيحرّكه قد إيه. الـ timeline cache: 7.0 TB لـ 800 عنصر لكل user، على timeline مقاس حوالي 69.6 KB. والـ post store 3 TB في السنة قبل الـ replicas والـ media.

اختيار التخزين. الـ posts والـ follows هما مصدر الحقيقة في Postgres، والـ timelines cache مشتق في Redis تقدر تبنيه تاني، فلو ضاع بتخسر latency مش data. وجدول follows لازم يتعمله index في الاتجاهين: للأمام عشان تعرف بتتابع مين في الـ pull، وللخلف عشان تجيب الـ followers في الـ fan-out.

فين هستخدمه في شغلي؟ أي dashboard فيها "أحدث الطلبات" أو activity feed لعميل. نفس السؤال: بتجمع وقت القراية من كذا مصدر (pull بـ LATERAL و index صح)، ولا بتجهز الـ list لكل user وقت الكتابة (push في Redis)؟ لو الـ data صغيرة وعدد المصادر قليل، استعلام LATERAL مظبوط كفاية ومفيش داعي لـ Redis خالص.

Part 2 — Push vs pull, measured 🟡 Intermediateالجزء 2 — الـ push مقابل الـ pull بالقياس

English

Both models run here on a real Redis, on sorted sets scored by a millisecond timestamp. Push (fan-out on write) writes the post id into every follower's timeline, with a trim to keep the size bounded. Pull (fan-out on read) writes only to the author's list, and the reader fetches and merges every followed author's list. Pipelining is doing most of the work: one network round trip per batch rather than one per command, in batches of 1,000.

The numbers are medians on one Windows machine under load, over loopback, so treat them as ≈ and look at ratios. Push write cost grows linearly with followers (1.6 → 84 ms from 100 to 10,000) while its read cost stays flat at 0.1 ms. Pull is the mirror image: writing is one ZADD, and reading grows with the number of followees (1.5 → 74 ms). Note the x-axis differs: push cost scales with the author's followers, pull cost with the reader's followees. That asymmetry is the whole design.

Extrapolating to 50M followers. Assumptions: linear pipeline cost continues, one connection, one Redis node, nothing else running. Then one celebrity post takes about 7 minutes; 32 perfectly balanced shards would make it 13 seconds. Those numbers are optimistic bounds: a production fan-out runs over a real network, and it also has to read the follower list. The numbers don't argue that push is impossible; they argue that the cost lands on a queue that other posts share, which Part 6 measures.

Memory is the part people forget. At 800 entries a sorted set exceeds the default 128-entry listpack limit and becomes a skiplist, about 87 bytes per entry and 7 TB across 100M users. Raising zset-max-listpack-entries to 800 uses listpack at 27 bytes per entry, 3× less, and here the extra ZADD cost didn't show up at that size. Treat that as a result to re-measure on your own hardware.

How to run these lab.ts reads the port of a private Redis from redis.port, and refuses a default or empty value, so nothing here can reach your real Redis by accident. Start your own disposable Redis on a free port and put that number in the file. The Postgres scripts use a throwaway-cluster helper from this repo's lab; any disposable database works, provided you pass the port explicitly.

// الـ port بييجي من ملف بيكتبه الـ Redis التجريبي، وأي قيمة مش صح بتوقف الـ script
export function checkedPort(raw: string | undefined): number {
  const n = Number(raw);
  if (!/^\d+$/.test(String(raw)) || n < 1024 || n > 65535 || [27017, 5432, 6379].includes(n)) {
    throw new Error("refusing port " + raw);
  }
  return n;
}

export function mulberry32(seed: number): () => number {
  let a = seed >>> 0;
  return () => {
    a = (a + 0x6d2b79f5) >>> 0;
    let t = a;
    t = Math.imul(t ^ (t >>> 15), t | 1);
    t ^= t + Math.imul(t ^ (t >>> 7), t | 61);
    return ((t ^ (t >>> 14)) >>> 0) / 4294967296;
  };
}

// الدالتين دول الـ push والـ pull الأساسيين، وكل الأجزاء اللي جاية بتستخدمهم
export type Entry = { id: string; ts: number };
export const CAP = 800;
const BATCH = 1000;

export async function fanOutPost(redis: Redis, followers: number[], e: Entry): Promise<void> {
  for (let i = 0; i < followers.length; i += BATCH) {
    const p = redis.pipeline();
    for (const u of followers.slice(i, i + BATCH)) {
      p.zadd(`tl:${u}`, e.ts, e.id);
      p.zremrangebyrank(`tl:${u}`, 0, -(CAP + 1));
    }
    await p.exec();
  }
}

export async function pullFeed(redis: Redis, authors: number[], limit = 20): Promise<Entry[]> {
  const p = redis.pipeline();
  for (const a of authors) p.zrevrange(`posts:${a}`, 0, limit - 1, "WITHSCORES");
  const res = (await p.exec()) as [Error | null, string[]][];
  const all: Entry[] = [];
  for (const [, flat] of res) {
    for (let i = 0; i < flat.length; i += 2) all.push({ id: flat[i], ts: Number(flat[i + 1]) });
  }
  return all.sort((a, b) => b.ts - a.ts || (a.id < b.id ? 1 : -1)).slice(0, limit);
}
import { newRedis, median, ms, fanOutPost, pullFeed, CAP } from "./lab.ts";

const redis = newRedis();
const REPS = 15;
await redis.flushdb();

// الـ pipeline أهم من أي حاجة هنا، من غيره كل ZADD بيدفع round trip كامل
const followers = (n: number) => Array.from({ length: n }, (_, i) => i + 1);

// الـ push: الكتابة بتكبر مع عدد الـ followers، والقراية ثابتة
async function push(n: number) {
  const list = followers(n);
  const writes: number[] = [];
  for (let r = 0; r < REPS; r++) {
    const t = process.hrtime.bigint();
    await fanOutPost(redis, list, { id: `p${n}_${r}`, ts: 1_780_000_000_000 + r });
    writes.push(ms(t));
  }
  const reads: number[] = [];
  for (let r = 0; r < 200; r++) {
    const t = process.hrtime.bigint();
    await redis.zrevrange("tl:1", 0, 19);
    reads.push(ms(t));
  }
  return { post: median(writes), read: median(reads) };
}

// الـ pull: الكتابة حركة واحدة، والقراية بتكبر مع عدد اللي بتتابعهم
async function pull(n: number) {
  const p = redis.pipeline();
  for (let a = 1; a <= n; a++) for (let k = 0; k < 5; k++) p.zadd(`posts:${a}`, 1_780_000_000_000 + a * k, `a${a}_${k}`);
  await p.exec();
  const authors = followers(n);
  const writes: number[] = [];
  for (let r = 0; r < 200; r++) {
    const t = process.hrtime.bigint();
    await redis.zadd(`posts:1`, 1_780_000_100_000 + r, `new_${r}`);
    writes.push(ms(t));
  }
  const reads: number[] = [];
  for (let r = 0; r < REPS; r++) {
    const t = process.hrtime.bigint();
    await pullFeed(redis, authors);
    reads.push(ms(t));
  }
  return { post: median(writes), read: median(reads) };
}

Part 3 — Hybrid and the threshold 🟡 Intermediateالجزء 3 — الـ hybrid ونقطة الـ threshold

English

Hybrid: fan out on write for ordinary accounts, pull for accounts above a follower threshold, and merge at read. The open question is where to set the threshold, and the honest answer depends on the follower distribution, which is skewed. The simulation below draws 100 million accounts from a capped Pareto with a seed, assumes large accounts post more often, and counts Redis operations a day at each threshold. It sends nothing and does not capture hot keys, batching, or how follows cluster per reader.

The table is the point. Pull-all costs 200B operations a day, almost all reads. Raising the threshold to 1M drops the total to 25B, and from 1M upward the total stops improving: writes rise as fast as reads fall, while the worst single fan-out goes from 1M to 50M. That flat part is the knee. Two practical conclusions: 1M is the threshold if operations are what you pay for, and 100k if single-post lag matters more, which costs 41% more operations for a worst fan-out ten times smaller. One more thing the averages hid: the simple calculator said 4B fan-out writes a day, and the skewed version, with heavy accounts posting more, says 12B ZADD (24B operations with trims).

Then the real run: 250 followed accounts, of which 50 are "celebrities". The hybrid merge returns the same top 20 as a full pull over all 250, which is what you must assert before trusting any merge logic. Merge latency grows from 0.2 ms with no celebrities to 1.4 ms with 50, a fine trade for a read that also saves every celebrity's fan-out. The simulation's average of 11 pulled accounts per read sits well inside that range.

Part 4 — Timeline cache and pagination 🟡 Intermediateالجزء 4 — الـ timeline cache والـ pagination

English

Cap the timeline. The figure widely quoted from Twitter's timeline talks is about 800 entries per user, and it's a useful default: older pages fall back to a pull. ZADD followed by ZREMRANGEBYRANK keeps the set at 800. Skip inactive users on fan-out: if only 30% of followers were active recently, 70% of the writes are wasted, and an inactive user rebuilds their timeline by pull on next login. On that seeded run 7,050 of 10,000 writes disappeared.

Offset pagination repeats posts when new ones arrive. After page 1, five new posts pushed the window so page 2 started with five posts already shown. The fix is a cursor by score: "everything strictly older than this timestamp", which doesn't move no matter what is inserted in front.

A score-only cursor has its own bug: many posts can share a millisecond, and the exclusive bound skips all of them. The tie test returned 0 of the remaining 15. The fix is a composite cursor of score and post id, using the fact that Redis orders equal scores by member. Don't try to avoid this by using a 64-bit Snowflake as the score: a sorted-set score is a double, so 1234567890123456789 reads back as 1234567890123456768.

Deleted posts and muted users are filtered at read time rather than at write time. A delete would otherwise be another full fan-out, and a mute is per-user anyway. Because filtering shrinks the page, the read loop over-fetches until the page is full. A background ZREM job can then clean up, but the read path can't depend on it.

Part 5 — Ranking 🟡 Intermediateالجزء 5 — الـ ranking

English

Ranking runs in two stages: candidate generation (your timeline plus followed celebrities, some hundreds of ids) then scoring. A simple scorer combines recency with a half-life, engagement on a log scale, and affinity to the author, each normalised to 0–1. Normalising matters: without it, raw like counts dwarf every other weight, and the test shows viral_old winning even with recency weighted 1,000×. With it, changing weights changes the order in the expected way, and that's what the assertions pin down.

Precompute or score at read? Slow-moving features such as affinity and engagement can be precomputed in a batch and stored in a feature store; fresh signals have to be read at request time. Most systems do both, and scoring itself is cheap. The latency cost is in fetching features. Measured on this Redis, 500 candidates take a median of 3.0 ms to fetch features and 0.11 ms to score, against a 40 ms budget slot; scale that by real network distance and p99 spread before relying on it.

Real ranking models are named here only: gradient-boosted trees over hand-built features were the common baseline, and large platforms use deep two-tower retrieval plus deep ranking models. The X (Twitter) recommendation pipeline is public in the-algorithm. In an interview, describe the two stages, the features and the budget, then say plainly that the model itself is a separate problem.

export type Candidate = {
  id: string;
  ageHours: number;
  likes: number;
  comments: number;
  // القرب من صاحب البوست، رقم من 0 لـ 1 بييجي من تفاعلك معاه
  affinity: number;
};
export type Weights = { recency: number; engagement: number; affinity: number };

// الوزن ده نصه بيروح كل 6 ساعات، فالبوست عمره 6 ساعات بياخد نص النقاط
const HALF_LIFE_HOURS = 6;
const recency = (c: Candidate) => Math.pow(0.5, c.ageHours / HALF_LIFE_HOURS);

function engagementRaw(c: Candidate) {
  return Math.log1p(c.likes + 2 * c.comments);
}

// ❌ النسخة دي من غير توحيد مقياس، بنستخدمها في الـ test بس عشان نشوف الغلطة
export function rankRaw(cands: Candidate[], w: Weights) {
  return cands
    .map((c) => ({ id: c.id, score: w.recency * recency(c) + w.engagement * (c.likes + 2 * c.comments) + w.affinity * c.affinity }))
    .sort((a, b) => b.score - a.score);
}

// كل مكوّن بيتظبط بين 0 و 1، وإلا اللايكات تبلع أي وزن تاني
export function rank(cands: Candidate[], w: Weights) {
  const maxE = Math.max(1, ...cands.map(engagementRaw));
  return cands
    .map((c) => ({
      id: c.id,
      score: w.recency * recency(c) + w.engagement * (engagementRaw(c) / maxE) + w.affinity * c.affinity,
    }))
    .sort((a, b) => b.score - a.score || (a.id < b.id ? 1 : -1));
}

Measure each stage against its budget slot, cap the candidate count, batch and precompute slow features. Scoring itself is rarely the cost.

الإجابة: جلب الـ features لكل مرحلة، وغالبًا هو نداء واحد ضخم لكل candidate على store بعيد في الـ p99. قيس كل مرحلة قصاد الـ slot بتاعها، وحط حد لعدد الـ candidates، واعمل batch وحسابات مسبقة للـ features البطيئة. والـ scoring نفسه نادرًا ما يكون هو التكلفة.

Part 6 — Trade-offs and review 🟡 Intermediateالجزء 6 — الـ trade-offs والمراجعة

English

Fan-out queue lag after a celebrity post. The simulation is virtual-time and seeded, on the measured batch cost plus an assumed follower-list read. With one shared FIFO queue, one 50M post at t=1 s delays 17.9% of every other post by more than a second, and the p99 reaches 32 seconds. Separate lanes for large accounts remove the lag for normal posts entirely, at the cost of the celebrity's fan-out taking 226 s instead of 35 s. Hybrid removes that work altogether. This is the same bulkhead idea as per-channel queues in the notification case.

Hot keys. A followed celebrity's list posts:{id} is read by a large share of all feed requests, so it becomes a hot key: at 34,722 reads/s, an account followed by half of all users would see about 17,000 reads/s on one key and one shard. Mitigations live in the hot-keys lesson, and the local-cache one applies directly here: a process cache of a few seconds for celebrity lists turns thousands of readers into a handful of Redis reads per process.

What breaks first at 10× (1B DAU): timeline memory, 70 TB at 800 entries, which forces a lower cap, listpack encoding, or an active-only cache. Then total operations: 250B a day is about 2.9M/s on average, and several shards' worth per peak. Then hot celebrity keys, then the follower index that fan-out scans.

const SIM_SECONDS = 240;
const NORMAL_RATE = 694;
const WORKERS = 20;
// زمن الـ batch ده من القياس: 1000 follower في حوالي 8.5ms، زايد قراية من الـ DB
const BATCH_MS = 13.5;
const NORMAL_JOB_MS = 7;
const CELEB_FOLLOWERS = 50_000_000;

type Job = { arrival: number; serviceMs: number; celeb: boolean };

function arrivals(rand: () => number): number[] {
  const out: number[] = [];
  let t = 0;
  while (t < SIM_SECONDS * 1000) {
    t += -Math.log(1 - rand()) * (1000 / NORMAL_RATE);
    out.push(t);
  }
  return out;
}

// الـ workers كلهم على طابور واحد FIFO، فالشغل بيتعمل بترتيب الوصول
function runFifo(jobs: Job[], workerCount: number) {
  const free = new Float64Array(workerCount);
  const finish: number[] = [];
  for (const job of jobs) {
    let idx = 0;
    for (let w = 1; w < workerCount; w++) if (free[w] < free[idx]) idx = w;
    const start = Math.max(free[idx], job.arrival);
    free[idx] = start + job.serviceMs;
    finish.push(free[idx]);
  }
  return finish;
}

function report(label: string, jobs: Job[], finish: number[]) {
  const lags: number[] = [];
  let celebDone = 0;
  jobs.forEach((j, i) => {
    if (j.celeb) celebDone = Math.max(celebDone, finish[i]);
    else lags.push(finish[i] - j.arrival);
  });
  const delayed = lags.filter((l) => l > 1000).length;
  lags.sort((a, b) => a - b);
  const p = (q: number) => lags[Math.min(lags.length - 1, Math.floor(q * lags.length))];
  console.log(`${label.padEnd(34)} p50 ${p(0.5).toFixed(0).padStart(5)} ms  p99 ${p(0.99).toFixed(0).padStart(6)} ms  max ${p(1).toFixed(0).padStart(6)} ms  posts delayed over 1 s ${(delayed / lags.length * 100).toFixed(1).padStart(5)}%  celeb done ${celebDone === 0 ? "n/a" : (celebDone / 1000).toFixed(0) + " s"}`);
}

const rand = mulberry32(50);
const normalArrivals = arrivals(rand);
const celebBatches: Job[] = Array.from({ length: Math.ceil(CELEB_FOLLOWERS / 1000) }, () => ({ arrival: 1000, serviceMs: BATCH_MS, celeb: true }));
const normalJobs: Job[] = normalArrivals.map((arrival) => ({ arrival, serviceMs: NORMAL_JOB_MS, celeb: false }));

{

}

بالعامية

لاج الـ fan-out queue بعد بوست مشهور. الـ simulation بوقت افتراضي و seed، على تكلفة الـ batch المقاسة زايد قراية مفترضة لـ list الـ followers. طابور FIFO واحد: بوست واحد لـ 50 مليون عند t=1 s بيأخر 17.9% من باقي البوستات أكتر من ثانية، والـ p99 بيوصل 32 ثانية. الـ lanes المنفصلة للحسابات الكبيرة بتشيل اللاج عن البوستات العادية خالص، والتمن إن fan-out المشهور بياخد 226 ثانية بدل 35. والـ hybrid بيلغي الشغل ده كله. دي نفس فكرة الـ bulkhead بتاعة الـ queues لكل channel في درس الـ notifications.

إيه اللي بيكسر الأول عند 10 أضعاف (مليار user نشط): ذاكرة الـ timelines 70 TB عند 800 عنصر، وده بيجبرك على cap أقل أو listpack أو cache للنشطين بس. بعدها مجموع العمليات: 250B في اليوم يعني حوالي 2.9 مليون في الثانية متوسط، وكذا shard للـ peak. وبعدها الـ hot keys بتاعة المشاهير، وبعدها الـ follower index اللي الـ fan-out بيمشي عليه.

Cheat sheet · الخلاصة

  • Pay at write (push) or at read (pull); the axes differ: followers vs followees.
  • Peak here: 34,722 reads/s, 694 posts/s, 139k push writes/s, 7M pull reads/s.
  • Measured at 10k: push post about 84 ms, pull read about 74 ms, other side about 0.1 ms.
  • Hybrid: fan out normal accounts, pull above a threshold, merge at read, assert the merge equals a full pull.
  • Knee on the seeded run: total ops stop improving at 1M; worst fan-out keeps growing. 100k costs +41% ops for a 10× smaller worst case.
  • Memory: 800 entries is a skiplist, about 87 B each and 7 TB. Listpack is 27 B each, re-measure on your hardware.
  • Cap at 800, skip inactive followers, rebuild on login by pull.
  • Cursor by (score, id) for ties; never offset; never a 64-bit id as the score.
  • Deleted and muted are filtered at read; over-fetch within a bounded loop.
  • Ranking: candidates, then scoring with normalised components and a latency budget per stage.
  • Separate fan-out lanes for big accounts: a shared queue left a p99 of about 32 s in the simulation.
  • At 10×: timeline memory first, then total Redis ops, then celebrity hot keys.
