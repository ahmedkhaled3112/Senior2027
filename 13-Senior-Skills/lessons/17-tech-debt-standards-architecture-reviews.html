Why this matters · ليه ده مهم

English

Nobody files a ticket called "keep this codebase healthy". Debt, new technology, standards and design reviews all sit in the gap between engineering and management, so either someone decides to own them or they drift. This is where a senior's judgement shows most clearly: which debt to pay, what to refuse, which boring thing to insist on, and how hard to push on a colleague's design. In interviews it comes up as "how did you convince leadership?". At work it comes up as a conversation about time and budget.

Everything in this lesson's scenarios is invented for teaching: the Orders team at a mid-size online store, the people (Mona the product manager, Hany the engineering manager, Karim a mid-level engineer, Salma a junior, Nour on infra, Yasmin the support lead) and every number. The mid-versus-senior contrasts are teaching patterns, not measurements. "You" is you, in that team.

Part 1 — Debt as a portfolio 🔴 Advancedالجزء 1 — الـ debt كـ portfolio

English

The situation. It's November and Hany is building the Q1 plan. He asks in the team channel: "If you had some engineering time next quarter, what would you do with it?" Karim says the code is a mess. You feel the same way, and a feeling doesn't get budget.

What a mid-level engineer does. Says "Everything in this codebase is bad, we need a refactor quarter", or pastes a 40-item list. Hany can't fund "bad", and 40 items give him nothing to choose between, so the answer becomes "let's see after the launch".

What a senior does. Picks three. For each one: what it cost last quarter in things Hany already counts (incidents, late tickets, support load), what the fix costs, and the order. Then the part people skip: what won't be fixed this year. Then asks for a decision by a date.

Why the second works. A manager can say yes to a bet with a price on it. Interest stated in incidents and late tickets is an argument about money and delivery, which is how Martin Fowler says the case has to be made: "we should only approach it as an economic argument". The won't-fix list is what makes the memo believable. Fowler's own example of debt not worth paying is a flaw "in a rarely touched part of the code-base", because the interest is small. Will Larson starts in the same place: measure the problem, find "where the bulk of the issue occurs", and work on that. He also calls low quality "the expected, normal state" at a successful company, so the memo doesn't need a villain.

The register that produces numbers like these is in Architecture lesson 09, part 3, and Fowler's quadrant is in part 1 of that lesson. This part is about the memo and the conversation.

To: Hany
From: You
Date: Mon 2 Nov 2026
Subject: Orders debt: the 3 that cost us most, and a 9-week ask for Q1

TL;DR: Three debts cost us 4 incidents and about 31 engineer-days in Q3. Fixing
all three costs 9 engineer-weeks. I'm asking for one engineer for most of Q1.
I'm not asking for a refactor quarter.

Where the numbers come from: the Q3 incident log, ticket history, git log and
Yasmin's support tags. Day counts are rough.

| Order | Debt                           | Q3 interest              | Fix     |
|-------|--------------------------------|--------------------------|---------|
| 1     | payment-webhook not idempotent | 3 incidents, ~11 days    | 3 weeks |
| 2     | invoice-pdf: no retries or DLQ | 1 incident, ~8 days      | 2 weeks |
| 3     | Order status logic in 3 places | 5 late tickets, ~12 days | 4 weeks |

1. payment-webhook (first: it's the only one that loses money)
   A resent event gets processed twice. In August that meant 2 double refunds,
   7,400 EGP. All the work happens inside the HTTP request, so one slow provider
   call backs up every webhook behind it (incidents on 12 Aug and 3 Sep).
   Fix: an inbox table with a unique event ID, then process in a BullMQ job.

2. invoice-pdf queue (second: cheap, and support feels it every day)
   Failed jobs disappear. 46 "where's my invoice" tickets in Q3. Someone re-runs
   them by hand, about 3 hours a week.
   Fix: retries with backoff, a dead-letter queue, an alert on its size.

3. Order status logic (third: the biggest, but nothing is on fire)
   The status rules live in POST /orders, the webhook handler and the Next.js
   admin. That code changed in 41 of 67 Orders PRs, and 5 tickets ran at least
   2 days late because of it.
   Fix: one OrderStatus module that all three call.

Won't fix in 2027:
- The old admin table component. Ugly, and changed twice in 12 months.
- The 2023 coupon importer. Worst code we own. It runs once a year and works.
- Replacing the ORM. No incident or late ticket traces back to it.

How we'll know it worked (review on 31 Mar):
- No double refunds. Webhook incidents go from 3 a quarter to 0 or 1.
- "Where's my invoice" tickets go from 46 a quarter to under 10.
- Tickets that touch order status stop running late for that reason.

Decision I need by Thu 12 Nov, for Q1 planning:
A. All three, 9 weeks. This is my recommendation.
B. #1 and #2 only, 5 weeks. The status logic keeps costing ~12 days a quarter.
C. None of them. Expect about the same 31 days again, more if orders grow.

الـ register اللي بيطلّع الأرقام دي موجود في درس Architecture 09، الجزء 3، والـ quadrant بتاع Fowler في الجزء 1 من نفس الدرس. هنا الكلام عن الـ memo والكلام مع مديرك.

الـ memo زي ما هيتكتب عندنا:

Part 2 — "We don't need that" 🔴 Advancedالجزء 2 — "مش محتاجين ده"

English

The situation. Karim posts a doc proposing Kafka for order events. The Orders API produces about 50 events a minute. He spent his weekend on the doc and he's excited about it.

What a mid-level engineer does. Either says yes because it's interesting and would look good on a CV, or sneers in the channel: "Kafka for 50 events a minute, seriously?" The first adds a system nobody can run. The second teaches Karim to stop proposing things.

What a senior does. Asks two questions: "What problem do we have today that BullMQ doesn't solve? At what volume would that change?" Then writes the answer down as a trigger, so "no" becomes "not yet, and here's what would change my mind".

Why the second works. Those questions are Dan McKinley's method in Choose Boring Technology: think about how you'd solve the immediate problem without adding anything new, and if the real problem is that someone wants to use the technology, stop there. His reason is operational. A boring tool's failure modes are well understood, and "the long-term costs of keeping a system working reliably vastly exceed any inconveniences you encounter while building it". His model is that a company gets "about three innovation tokens", which he calls approximate. Fifty events a minute is under one a second, and System Design lesson 44, part 3 measures how far much plainer tools go. The trigger moves the argument from taste to a number anyone can check.

شجرة قرار لأي تكنولوجيا جديدة، بنقراها من فوق لتحت. أول سؤال: عندنا مشكلة النهارده؟ لو لأ، الرد "مش دلوقتي" ونكتب الـ trigger اللي يرجّعنا للموضوع ونقف. لو أيوه: اللي عندنا يقدر يحلها بتكلفة مقبولة؟ لو أيوه استخدمه حتى لو أقل شياكة. لو لأ، نكتب بالظبط ليه الـ stack الحالي هيخلي الحل غالي. بعدها: عندنا innovation token فاضي؟ لو لأ، نشيل حاجة الأول أو نستنى. بعدها: مين هيشغّلها الساعة 3 الفجر؟ لو مفيش حد، نقف لحد ما يتسمّى حد. لو فيه owner: تجربة بوقت محدد على مسار مش حرج مع طريق رجوع. لو X هتحل محل حاجة موجودة، نلتزم بالـ migration بميعاد. ولو لأ، نعتمدها ونسجل القرار في ADR.
Karim, thanks for writing this up. The section on replay is the clearest
explanation anyone has given of our ordering problem, and I want to keep it.

Before we add anything to the stack, three questions:

1. What problem do we have today that BullMQ doesn't solve? I can see two in
   your doc: replaying events after a bad deploy, and a second consumer for
   analytics. Is there a third?
2. We're at about 50 events a minute, which is less than one a second. At what
   volume, or what new need, would the answer change?
3. Who's on call for it? Nour is our only infra engineer, and none of us has run
   Kafka in production.

Where I land today: not now. For replay, an order_events table in Postgres gives
us a log we can read again from any point, written in the same transaction as
the order. That's about a week of work and adds nothing new to run.

What would reopen this. Any one of these, measured rather than predicted:
- A second team needs the same events independently, and reading the table
  isn't enough for them.
- Sustained peak goes above 200 events a second (about 240x today). Nour will
  load-test the current setup in Q1 so that number is ours and not a guess.
- Replaying from the table takes longer than our 30-minute recovery target.

If one of those fires, I'd like you to lead that RFC, starting from this doc.

In the meantime, would you take the order_events table? It's the same ideas
(ordered log, consumer position, replay), it's useful now, and it's the piece
that would feed Kafka later if we ever get there.

الموقف. كريم نزّل doc بيقترح Kafka للـ order events. الـ Orders API بيطلّع حوالي 50 event في الدقيقة. وهو قعد في الـ weekend يكتبه ومتحمس.

الـ Mid بيعمل إيه. يا إما يقول أيوه عشان الموضوع حلو وشكله كويس في الـ CV، يا إما يتريق في الـ channel: "Kafka لـ 50 event في الدقيقة؟ بجد؟". الأولى بتضيف system محدش عارف يشغّله. والتانية بتعلّم كريم يبطّل يقترح.

السنيور بيعمل إيه. بيسأل سؤالين: "إيه المشكلة اللي عندنا النهارده وBullMQ مش بيحلها؟ وعند أنهي volume ده يتغير؟". وبعدين يكتب الإجابة كـ trigger، فكلمة "لأ" تبقى "مش دلوقتي، ودي الحاجة اللي تغيّر رأيي".

ليه ده بينجح. السؤالين دول هما طريقة Dan McKinley في Choose Boring Technology: فكّر هتحل المشكلة الحالية إزاي من غير ما تضيف أي حاجة جديدة، ولو طلعت المشكلة الحقيقية إن حد نفسه يستخدم التكنولوجيا، اقف هنا. وسببه تشغيلي: الأداة المملة الـ failure modes بتاعتها معروفة، وتكلفة إنك تخلي system شغال بشكل موثوق على المدى الطويل أكبر بكتير من أي مضايقة وإنت بتبنيه ("vastly exceed"، زي ما كتب). ونموذجه إن الشركة ليها "about three innovation tokens"، يعني حوالي 3 توكنز للتجديد، وهو نفسه بيقول النموذج تقريبي. و50 event في الدقيقة أقل من واحد في الثانية، ودرس System Design 44، الجزء 3 بيقيس حاجات أبسط بكتير بتوصل لحد فين. والـ trigger بينقل النقاش من الذوق لرقم أي حد يقدر يتأكد منه.

الرد زي ما هيتكتب عندنا:

يا كريم، تسلم إيدك على الـ doc، وجزء الـ replay بالذات أوضح شرح حد قاله لمشكلة الـ ordering عندنا، وعايز نحتفظ بيه. قبل ما نضيف أي حاجة للـ stack عندي 3 أسئلة:

  1. إيه المشكلة اللي عندنا النهارده وBullMQ مش بيحلها؟ أنا شايف اتنين في الـ doc بتاعك: replay للـ events بعد deploy بايظ، وconsumer تاني للـ analytics. فيه تالتة؟
  2. إحنا عند حوالي 50 event في الدقيقة، يعني أقل من واحد في الثانية. عند أنهي volume، أو أنهي احتياج جديد، الإجابة تتغير؟
  3. مين هيبقى on-call عليه؟ نور هو الـ infra engineer الوحيد عندنا، ومحدش فينا شغّل Kafka في production قبل كده.

Part 6 — Standards that enforce themselves 🔴 Advancedالجزء 6 — standards بتطبّق نفسها

English

The situation. The team's wiki has a 40-rule coding standards page, last edited 14 months ago. Salma, in her first month, asks which of the rules actually matter. Nobody can say.

What a mid-level engineer does. Adds rule 41, or repeats the rules as review comments on every PR.

What a senior does. Cuts it down to a short Definition of Done and a short guidelines page, with each line marked automated or human. Anything a machine can check goes to a machine: ESLint and Prettier, CI, CODEOWNERS, architecture tests (the CI side is in Node lesson 32). What's left is what only a person can judge, and it's short enough to remember. Adopts it with a proposal and a trial. Deletes rules that don't pay. The review rules in the table come from lesson 05.

Why the second works. In Manage technical quality, Larson writes: "Deliberate tools create workflows that nurture habits far better than training and documentation." He tells a story of Scrum being declared on a wiki while nobody's work changed, which is the 40-rule page exactly. His adoption advice is that "a good process is evolved rather than mandated": try it with a few engaged people, fix the rough edges, and roll out one practice at a time so you can tell what helped. A rule that a machine enforces also stops being a personal remark in a review.

Hint · تلميح Before you send the memo, ask your manager what they count. If they care about delivery dates more than incidents, lead with the late tickets. Don't send a memo with numbers you can't defend.
قبل ما تبعت الـ memo، اسأل مديرك بيعدّ إيه أصلًا. لو مهتم بمواعيد التسليم أكتر من الـ incidents، ابدأ بالـ tickets المتأخرة. ومتبعتش memo فيه أرقام مش عارف تدافع عنها.
Solution · الحل Worked example from the invented team: reviewing Karim's refunds module with questions only, the first comment was "A refund sits in pending_provider for 2 days. What does Yasmin's team see?" Karim hadn't considered it, and added a status page for support himself. Then the review asks about R1 as a question: "What stops one provider refund from creating two rows?" A question can carry a blocking mark too.
مثال من الفريق المتألف: في review الـ refunds module بأسئلة بس، أول comment كان "refund قعد في pending_provider يومين، فريق ياسمين هيشوف إيه؟". كريم مكانش فكر فيها، وعمل بنفسه صفحة status للـ support. وبعدها الـ review سأل عن R1 كسؤال: "إيه اللي يمنع إن refund واحد من الـ provider يعمل صفين؟". والسؤال ممكن يبقى عليه علامة blocking برضه.

Cheat sheet · الخلاصة

  • Debt: pick 3. Interest in things your manager counts, a fix cost, an order, and what you won't fix. Ask for a decision by a date.
  • Make the case as economics, not professionalism. Nobody can fund "bad".
  • New tech: "What problem do we have today that the current stack doesn't solve? At what volume does that change?"
  • A "not now" needs a measurable trigger and another way for the proposer to learn.
  • Boring work: rebuild the last 3 incidents minute by minute. Say where it wouldn't have helped.
  • Past incidents can't justify everything. For a loss that hasn't happened, argue from its size and say so.
  • Delivery vs quality: cut scope first. Deliberate debt needs what's skipped, the risk, who accepted it, a ticket and a date.
  • Reviews: questions, risks, alternatives, each advisory or blocking. Block only one-way doors and security.
  • Advisory means some things ship that you'd have done differently. That's the price of people who own decisions.
  • Standards: machines check what machines can. The human list stays short.
  • Adopt with a proposal and a trial, one change at a time. Delete rules that don't pay.
بالعامية
  • الـ debt: اختار 3. الفايدة بالحاجات اللي مديرك بيعدّها، وسعر التصليح، والترتيب، واللي مش هتصلّحه. واطلب قرار بميعاد.
  • اعرض القضية كحساب اقتصادي مش كاحتراف ومبادئ. محدش يقدر يموّل كلمة "وحش".
  • التكنولوجيا الجديدة: "إيه المشكلة اللي عندنا النهارده والـ stack الحالي مش بيحلها؟ وعند أنهي volume ده يتغير؟".
  • "مش دلوقتي" محتاجة trigger يتقاس، وطريقة تانية اللي اقترح يتعلم بيها.
  • الشغل الممل: ارجع لآخر 3 incidents دقيقة بدقيقة، وقول فين مكانش هيفيد.
  • الـ incidents اللي فاتت مش بتبرر كل حاجة. لو الخسارة لسه محصلتش، اتكلم عن حجمها وقول إن دي حجتك.
  • الـ delivery قصاد الـ quality: قلّل الـ scope الأول. والـ debt المقصود محتاج: إيه اللي اتساب، الخطر، مين وافق، ticket، وميعاد.
  • الـ reviews: أسئلة، مخاطر، بدائل، وكل واحد advisory أو blocking. والـ block على الـ one-way doors والـ security بس.
  • الـ advisory معناه إن حاجات هتطلع إنت كنت هتعملها بشكل تاني. وده تمن إن يبقى عندك ناس بتمتلك قراراتها.
  • الـ standards: الـ machines تتأكد من اللي تقدر عليه، وقايمة الـ human تفضل قصيرة.
  • اعتمد بـ proposal وتجربة، وتغيير واحد في المرة. وامسح الـ rules اللي مش بتجيب نتيجة.
