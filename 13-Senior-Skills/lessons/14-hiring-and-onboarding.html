Why this matters · ليه ده مهم

English

Who joins a team shapes it for years, and it's easy for that decision to rest on a job ad nobody reread and on whichever interviewer spoke first. Being "good at reading people" is not the skill here. The skill is building a process where the judgement can be checked: decide what you need before you see a CV, ask everyone the same questions, write down what was said, score before you talk, and then give the person you hired a first month with a plan in it. You don't need the title to start: offer to write the rubric or fix the setup doc.

This is the same story as the rest of the section: the Orders team at a mid-size online store, with Mona (product manager), Hany (engineering manager), Karim (mid-level), Salma (junior), Nour (infra) and Yasmin (support lead). The team, names and numbers in the scenarios are invented for teaching, and the Mid-versus-Senior contrasts are teaching patterns, not measurements. Anything here about hiring law is deliberately left to your HR team.

Part 1 — What do we actually need? 🔴 Advancedالجزء 1 — إحنا محتاجين إيه فعلًا؟

English

The situation. Hany has approval for one backend hire. The job ad from two years ago says "React, Node, AWS, 5+ years". Meanwhile, the incident list says the real gap is someone who has run Postgres in production and can own the queues.

What a mid-level engineer does. Copies the old job description, adds "Next.js" because the team uses it, and sends it to the recruiter. The candidates who show up match the ad perfectly and don't match the problem.

What a senior does. Writes a one-page hiring brief for Hany before anyone reads a CV: the gap, with data from the last two quarters; what this person owns after six months; a short list of must-haves, each of which the interview can actually test; nice-to-haves that can never be a reason to say no; and what was removed from the old ad and why.

Why the second works. The brief makes the team agree what good looks like while it's still cheap to disagree. It also becomes the input for Part 2, because every must-have has to map to an interview stage. If a must-have can't be tested in the loop, it isn't really a must-have.

# Hiring brief: backend engineer, Orders team
Author: You. For: Hany. 18 Oct 2026. Status: draft for discussion.

## The gap
6 production incidents in the last two quarters, 4 of them in Postgres or BullMQ: lock queues during a migration, a stuck `invoice-pdf` queue, and two slow queries. Nour is the only person who can tune either, and Nour is shared with three other teams. The old ad asks for none of this.

## What this person owns after 6 months
- The `invoice-pdf` and `notifications` queues: alerts, runbooks, capacity
- Postgres health for orders: slow-query review, safe migrations
- A normal place in the on-call rotation

## Must-haves (3, each tested in the loop)
1. Has operated Postgres in production: diagnosed a real slow query or lock problem
2. Has owned a background-job system through a failure, on any queue technology
3. Writes production Node.js and TypeScript without supervision

## Nice-to-haves (never a reason to reject)
- BullMQ specifically: we can teach it in two weeks
- Next.js: the admin is small
- Payments or webhooks

## Removed from the old ad, and why
- "5+ years": counts time, not whether they've done the things above. We test those instead
- "AWS": Nour owns the platform
- "React": about a tenth of this job, and Next.js is already a nice-to-have

## Interview loop (every stage tests a must-have)
| Stage                                      | Tests           | Who         |
|--------------------------------------------|-----------------|-------------|
| Owns production, 45 min                    | Must-haves 1, 2 | Nour, Karim |
| System design: order notifications, 60 min | Must-have 2     | You         |
| Practical coding on `POST /orders`, 60 min | Must-have 3     | Karim       |

## What I need from you, Hany
1. Is the level Senior only, or can we hire a strong Mid with a growth plan?
2. Karim and I need about 6 hours each over the next month. Can that come out of the sprint?

Part 2 — Structured interviews 🔴 Advancedالجزء 2 — الـ Structured interviews

English

The situation. Last month Karim and Nour interviewed the same candidate. Karim chatted about side projects, Nour asked about locks, and in the debrief Karim said "strong candidate, good vibes" and Nour said "not sure". Hany went with the louder opinion.

What a mid-level engineer does. Improvises the questions, trusts instinct, writes a sentence afterwards, and hears everyone else's opinion before writing it.

What a senior does. Writes the rubric first, asks every candidate the same questions in the same order, and looks for behaviours rather than impressions. The notes record what was said. Each interviewer writes their score and evidence alone, before the debrief, so the loudest voice in the room doesn't set the number.

Why the second works. There's research behind this. Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens (2022, Journal of Applied Psychology) re-analysed how well selection methods predict job performance. Structured interviews ranked at the top, and the validity estimates overall came out lower than earlier work assumed. So structure helps, and no method is a crystal ball. Will Larson's Staff-plus interview processes names the failure you're guarding against, "someone like us": debrief statements like "they feel like they'd be a natural part of this team", which anchor on how a person projects confidence rather than on what they can do. He's writing about Staff roles, and it applies at Senior too.

Rubric: Owns production (behavioural, 45 min)
Used for every candidate for this role. Written 20 Oct, before any CV.

Questions, same order, every candidate
  Q1. Tell me about the last production problem you were personally
      responsible for fixing.
  Q2. What did you look at first, and why?
  Q3. How did you know it was actually fixed?
  Q4. What changed afterwards, because of you?
  Follow-up if needed: "What did you do yourself, and what did others do?"

Scoring anchors (write the evidence first, then the number)
  1  No production story of their own. Talks about what the team did:
     "we" with no "I", even when asked.
  2  Has a story, but followed someone else's lead. Can't say how they
     knew it was fixed.
  3  Led the diagnosis with evidence (metrics, EXPLAIN, logs). Separates
     the mitigation from the cause. Names a follow-up that shipped.
  4  All of 3, and changed the system so this class of failure gets
     caught earlier. Says what they got wrong. Taught others.

  Bar for Senior on this team: 3.

Not evidence (never write these down as a reason)
  Confident, likeable, reminded me of someone, went to a good university,
  good vibes.

Example of a note that counts
  Q2: "Checked pg_stat_activity, saw one transaction holding a lock on
  orders for 40 s, traced it to a migration without a lock timeout."
  Score: 3. Led it with evidence. The follow-up was an alert on lock wait.

الموقف. الشهر اللي فات كريم ونور عملوا interview لنفس المرشح. كريم دردش عن الـ side projects، ونور سأل عن الـ locks، وفي الـ debrief كريم قال "مرشح قوي، حاسس بيه مظبوط" ونور قال "مش متأكد". هاني مشي مع الرأي الأعلى صوتًا.

السنيور بيعمل إيه. بيكتب الـ rubric الأول، ويسأل كل المرشحين نفس الأسئلة بنفس الترتيب، ويدوّر على السلوك مش الانطباع. والملاحظات بتسجل اللي اتقال. وكل interviewer بيكتب درجته والدليل لوحده قبل الـ debrief، عشان أعلى صوت في الأوضة ميحددش الرقم.

ليه ده بينجح. فيه بحث ورا ده. الـ Rubric بالعامية (بتاع "Owns production"، behavioural، 45 دقيقة، ومستخدم لكل مرشح لنفس الوظيفة واتكتب 20 أكتوبر قبل أي CV):

  • الأسئلة بنفس الترتيب: (1) احكيلي عن آخر مشكلة production كنت إنت شخصيًا مسؤول تصلحها. (2) بصيت على إيه الأول وليه؟ (3) عرفت منين إنها اتصلحت فعلًا؟ (4) إيه اللي اتغير بعدها بسببك؟ وسؤال متابعة لو احتجت: "إنت عملت إيه بنفسك، والباقي عملوا إيه؟"
  • درجة 1: معندوش قصة production بتاعته. بيحكي الفريق عمل إيه، "إحنا" من غير "أنا" حتى لما يتسأل.
  • درجة 2: عنده قصة بس كان ماشي ورا حد تاني. ومش عارف يقول عرف منين إنها اتصلحت.
  • درجة 3: قاد التشخيص بدليل (metrics أو EXPLAIN أو logs). بيفرّق بين الـ mitigation والسبب. وبيسمّي follow-up اتنفذ فعلًا.
  • درجة 4: كل اللي في 3، وغيّر السيستم عشان النوع ده من الـ failures يتمسك بدري. وبيقول غلط في إيه. وعلّم غيره.
  • الحد المطلوب للسنيور في الفريق ده: 3.
  • مش دليل (متتكتبش أبدًا كسبب): واثق من نفسه، لطيف، فكّرني بحد، خريج جامعة كويسة، "الـ vibes حلوة".
  • مثال لملاحظة بتتحسب: في السؤال 2 قال "شيّكت على pg_stat_activity ولقيت transaction ماسكة lock على orders لمدة 40 ثانية، ووصلت إنها migration من غير lock timeout". درجة 3: قاد الموضوع بدليل، والـ follow-up كان alert على الـ lock wait.
فين هستخدمه في شغلي؟ لو بتعمل interviews، أو هتبدأ: قبل المقابلة الجاية اكتب الأسئلة بالترتيب و4 مستويات للدرجة. وبعد المقابلة، اكتب درجتك قبل ما تفتح الشات مع باقي الفريق. لو إنت لسه مش بتعمل interviews، اطلب تحضر واحدة وتكتب درجتك لوحدك للمقارنة.

Part 3 — Evaluating design and coding 🔴 Advancedالجزء 3 — تقييم الـ design والـ coding

English

The situation. You're running the design interview: order notifications. Thirty-eight minutes in, the candidate has drawn a sensible queue and then goes quiet on what happens during a deploy.

What a mid-level engineer does. Either rescues them ("you'd handle SIGTERM here, right?") or lets them drown for fifteen minutes. For the coding round, picks a puzzle because it's easy to grade.

What a senior does. Lists the signals before the interview. Gives hints about the problem and never the solution, and writes every hint on the scorecard so the score can be read honestly. When someone's stuck: ask what they're considering, restate the question, narrow it to a smaller piece, offer one fact, and after a few minutes move on so that one stuck point doesn't eat the session.

Why the second works. A hint that names the answer tests whether they can agree with you. A hint that names the failure tests whether they can think. Writing hints down also stops the common debrief error of treating "got there after three hints" and "got there alone" as the same result. For coding, treat this as advice: a small exercise close to the real job says more than a puzzle. Larson, writing about Staff-plus candidates, says asking about their work is never as helpful as watching them do it, and Joel Spolsky's old test from 2000 still stands: do candidates write code in the interview? Agree in advance whether AI assistants are allowed in the coding round and tell every candidate the same thing, or you're comparing different tests. What a candidate should do in a design interview is in the design communication lesson, and section 14 (Interview Prep) is the candidate's side.

The situation. The debrief for C-117. Someone says "not senior enough", somebody else nods, and it's about to be over in four minutes with nothing written down.

English

English

The situation. Omar has his laptop and his accounts. On Sunday morning nobody has decided what he does next.

What a mid-level engineer does. Says "look around the code for a bit and let me know if you have questions". Omar reads files for two weeks, has no idea what matters, and is too new to say so.

What a senior does. Writes the plan before he arrives: day 1, week 1, month 1, with a named buddy, a first task sized to ship, a first on-call shadow and check-ins at weeks 1 and 4. The first task is real but small. He reads the last few postmortems because they explain how this system actually fails, and he gets a tour of Postgres and the queues from Nour.

Why the second works. A small shipped task in week one proves that the whole path works for this person: repo, review, CI, deploy. The SRE book's chapter on this argues for structure over chaos, and describes shadow on-call in a way worth copying: copy incoming pages to the new person, at first only during business hours, while someone else is responsible, because that removes the time pressure. Some of those teams then "reverse shadow", where the new person is primary and the experienced one quietly stays ready to help. That's Google's practice, so adapt the size to your team.

Cheat sheet · الخلاصة

  • Hiring brief first: the gap with data, what they own at 6 months, few must-haves each tested in the loop, nice-to-haves that never reject.
  • If a must-have has no interview stage that tests it, it's not a must-have.
  • Structured: rubric written first, same questions and order, notes record what was said, four anchors you could actually hear.
  • Scores and evidence written alone, before the debrief. Most junior first. A score changes only with new evidence.
  • "Good vibes" is not evidence. Watch for "someone like us".
  • Design: signals listed beforehand; hint at the failure, never the solution; record every hint with the minute; move on after a few minutes.
  • Coding, as advice: a practical exercise close to the job. Same exercise and same AI policy for every candidate.
  • Feedback in one sentence: evidence plus recommendation. To candidates, only after checking company policy and HR.
  • Never ask about age, religion, marital status, children, health or origin. Check local rules with HR.
  • docs/onboarding.md: accounts, three commands, test data, real first issues, who to ask. The newcomer's first PR fixes it.
  • First month: day 1, week 1, month 1, a buddy with time in the sprint, a task sized to ship, shadow on-call, check-ins at weeks 1 and 4.

  • لو must-have ملهاش مرحلة في الـ interview بتختبرها، يبقى مش must-have.
